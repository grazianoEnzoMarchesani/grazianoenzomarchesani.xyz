A façade is normally optimised for one thing at a time. Either you are
protecting the interior — reducing solar gains, cutting the cooling load,
keeping the glazing in shade — or you are working on the public space,
in which case you are usually thinking about trees, paving and water,
and the building is just the wall the street happens to have.

The paper we published in *Building and Environment* with Emanuele Naboni
and Roberta Cocci Grifoni looks at what happens when you treat those two
as one problem.

## The mechanism

In a hot urban canyon, a large share of the discomfort at street level is
radiant, not convective. The air temperature in the middle of a Southern
European street on an August afternoon is unpleasant; the mean radiant
temperature — what your body actually exchanges heat with — can be
fifteen degrees higher, because you are standing in the middle of a set
of hot surfaces radiating at you from every side.

The façade is one of those surfaces, and it is the biggest one.

A flat façade in the sun is a uniform radiant plate aimed at the
pedestrian. Give it three-dimensional relief — recesses, projections,
oriented fins, deep reveals — and two things happen at once. The building
shades itself, which is the effect everybody designs for. And the façade
partially shades *itself from the street's point of view*: part of what
the pedestrian sees is now surface in shadow, and part of the reflected
short-wave radiation is redirected upward rather than across.

## Why it is worth quantifying

The interesting part is that the two effects do not require the same
geometry. The relief that minimises cooling load is not automatically the
relief that minimises street-level MRT — the first wants shade on the
glazing, the second wants a low view factor of hot surface from
pedestrian height. Optimise for one and you get the other for free only
by accident.

So the work is a coupled evaluation: parametric façade variants,
simulated for both the building energy demand and the outdoor radiant
environment, and read together. The result is a set of geometries where
the two curves overlap — where a designer can buy street comfort without
paying for it in cooling load, and vice versa.

## The part that matters for practice

Façade relief is not an expensive intervention relative to what it is
compared with. Planting a canopy in a historic street often runs into
underground services, root space and heritage constraints; changing the
paving means closing the street. Reworking the geometry of a façade under
renovation is something that happens anyway, on a schedule that already
exists.

Which means this is one of the few adaptation measures that can be
attached to work already planned, rather than requiring its own budget
line. That is not a small property. Most of the microclimate literature
proposes interventions that are correct and unfunded.

The limit, stated plainly: the effect is local. A single treated façade
changes the comfort of the metres in front of it, not of the district.
It is a measure that becomes significant when it becomes a rule — a
requirement in a renovation code, not a gesture on one building.
