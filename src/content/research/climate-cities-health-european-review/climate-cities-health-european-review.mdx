# Climate, cities and health, mapped together

A changing climate, cities, health: three vast research fields, each with its own journals and conferences, that rarely talk to one another. Anyone wanting to study how adaptation and mitigation strategies applied to urban regeneration affect the health of city dwellers finds little literature to build on. The research group led by Ioanna Kyprianou, of the Cyprus Institute, set out to measure just how little, and to put it in order: 110 European publications, drawn from an initial pool of nearly 900, of which only one appeared before 2008 and half after 2018.

The stated aim is not to propose a new solution but to take stock of those already in circulation: which climate hazards urban research actually tackles, with which tools, and which effects on health it measures. Health, here, is understood in two ways — human health, as well-being or disease, and environmental health, as an urban ecosystem that thrives or declines.

## How the review was built

The selection of studies follows the PRISMA scheme, the standard for systematic reviews, paired with the CIMO logic (Context, Intervention, Mechanism, Outcome), used to translate the research question into queries. The question posed is: how can mitigation and adaptation strategies (Mechanism) offset the effects of climate change (Intervention) to improve health (Outcome) in urban contexts (Context)? For each of the four components the authors defined a list of keywords, combined with Boolean operators, and queried two databases with different vocations: Scopus, oriented towards engineering and the natural sciences, and PubMed, oriented towards medicine and health. A manual search for specific articles completed the queries, last run on 14 March 2022. The filtering path — removing duplicates, selecting English-language records and document types, restricting to European cities, reading the full text — leads from 879 database results plus 14 from other sources to the 110 final studies (**Table 1** and **Figure 1**).[^2] The restriction to Europe was imposed because the number of global results was otherwise too high, with some flexibility for particularly interesting work outside the Union.

For each of the four components the authors fixed a list of keywords (**Table 1**): curly braces enclose phrases searched as an exact string, the asterisk stands for truncation.

From here on the work is analysis, not simulation: the authors neither model nor measure anything themselves, they read and code what they found. Two tools guide the reading. The first is bibliometric co-occurrence analysis, carried out with VOSviewer 1.6.18, which identifies which terms often appear together in the keywords — those assigned by the authors and the index ones — drawing the map of dominant themes. The second is SWOT analysis: born as a strategic planning tool in the corporate world, here it is bent to another use, namely classifying each study by which aspects it highlights — strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats — and how often a given theme recurs.

## A young field skewed towards heat

The publication timeline tells of a recent field (**Figures 2 and 3**). Up to 2007 there is a single work meeting all three requirements; growth begins after 2008, with a clear dip in 2019 and a rebound in 2020. Half the output is later than 2018. The studies concentrate on one type of climate — temperate without a dry season, humid or oceanic, which applies to 49 of the 110 cases — and on intermediate scales, above all the city and the neighbourhood, far more often on open spaces than on enclosed ones.[^1]

The co-occurrence map confirms the priorities (**Figure 4**). ## Which hazards get studied

On the hazard front, research looks far more at events of human origin (103 studies) than at natural ones (90), but in both cases heat dominates (**Figure 5**). Among hazards tied to human activity, the urban heat island — the overheating of built-up areas relative to the surrounding countryside — appears in 64 studies, ahead of air, soil and water pollution (24) and shortage of energy or other resources (15). Among natural phenomena, heat stress comes first with 60 studies, followed by floods and heavy rainfall (17), disease outbreaks (8) and drought (5).

One figure stands out precisely because it is zero: dust storms are treated by none of the 110 studies. This is an effect of the European lens. In other parts of the world — the Eastern Mediterranean, the Gulf area — airborne dust is a front-rank health problem, but the review's geographical restriction cuts it out entirely. It is the kind of absence to keep in mind when reading a synthesis of this sort: it says what European research looks at, not what matters everywhere.

## Mitigation strategies

Strategies to reduce the causes of the problem concentrate on one clear area: urban design, land-use planning and direct action against the heat island, with 47 studies (**Figure 6**). Next comes the group of low-energy buildings and neighbourhoods (41 studies in total), driven by energy efficiency (21) and energy conservation (10), while smart and renewable technologies remain marginal. Two entire classes turn out to be little explored: sustainable mobility, with 20 studies spread evenly across modal shift, shared mobility, services and traffic optimisation, and above all the circular economy — carbon capture, low-carbon materials, material efficiency — stuck at 14 studies in all.

## Adaptation strategies

In adaptation the distribution is more balanced (**Figure 7**). The most studied group is policy-based strategies (87 studies), where design guidelines (37) and planning with zoning regulations (33) are almost on a par, while emergency risk reduction lags further behind (15) and insurance policies are almost absent (2 studies). Community-driven strategies follow close behind (79 studies). Ecosystem-based strategies (64 studies) are dominated by a single element: green spaces, with 48 studies, against just 1 devoted to the restoration of urban rivers or forests and 15 to green roofs and walls. Technology-based strategies close the list (53 studies), mostly climate-proof transport and infrastructure and water management.

## Where it lands: health

On the outcome side, human health is measured far more than environmental health (165 studies against 83), and within human health one theme prevails over all: thermal comfort, present in 80 studies (**Figure 8**). Physical disease (27), fitness (22) and mental health (22) are treated fairly evenly, while equity — who suffers the effects of heat most, and why — stops at 14 studies. On the environmental side, improved urban ecosystems (35) and resource-use efficiency (31) are more studied than the reduction of greenhouse-gas emissions (17).

## More strengths than threats, and why

Classifying the studies by the SWOT scheme, a marked imbalance emerges: 92 strengths and 96 opportunities, against 40 weaknesses and 35 threats (**Figure 9**). The authors attribute this to the way the research is built: the queries sought adaptation and mitigation projects, not the negative impacts of cities on health, and so the sample naturally leans towards what works. It is an honest caveat, and it changes how to read the rest: the imbalance does not say the research is optimistic, it says the optimistic part is what was searched for.

## When a solution backfires

The most useful part of the discussion concerns the cases in which a strategy, poorly applied, makes things worse. Solar-reflective pavements — often presented as a simple, cheap remedy against heat — do not help in very open streets, with a large share of visible sky: there the light reflected off the ground finds no walls to absorb it and ends up on passers-by, raising the mean radiant temperature, that is, the net radiation the body exchanges with the surrounding surfaces. The result is worse comfort during daytime hours precisely where relief was expected.

The second case is that of lock-ins. For the building sector of western Europe the estimated energy-saving potential exceeds 70%, but the share that remains unrealised — the lock-in risk — is around 45%: legacy infrastructure and carbon-intensive technologies that persist for decades and effectively hinder the transition. Even the isolated implementation of a single measure, disconnected from the others, can amplify the benefits or substantially reduce them. The recommendation that follows is to aim for a "malleable" urban design, one that acknowledges the impossibility of predicting cities' future needs exactly and leaves room for adjustment.

## What the literature doesn't say

Some absences are explicit. The participatory theme — involving citizens in the co-design of public spaces — is identified by the authors as the most important emerging strand, yet it remains poorly represented in the studies and does not appear among the frequent keywords. Water-related health problems, though relevant to urban life, find almost no room: the literature analysed names them but proposes no solutions.

Other limits are methodological and should be kept in mind when weighting the numbers. The review considers only English-language publications, only European cities, only two databases (plus a manual search), with the last update in March 2022. SWOT coding is a qualitative operation, hence partly subjective, and — as noted — the prevalence of strengths and opportunities is an effect of how the queries were built, not a property of the field.

## What to take away

The main recommendation the authors draw is a single one, and they state it plainly: to promote in regeneration projects a community-driven, inclusive and participatory approach, so that the different vulnerabilities are genuinely addressed and the different population groups obtain equitable health benefits. The two things this work makes it possible to do more concretely are: to know where research is already dense and where it is nearly empty — circular economy, urban rivers and forests, equity, the social dimension — and to recognise that some popular solutions, from cool pavements to measures applied one at a time, have a sign that depends on context and can create the very lock-ins they are meant to avoid.

# Clima, città e salute, mappati insieme

Clima che cambia, città, salute: tre ambiti di ricerca vastissimi, ciascuno con le sue riviste e i suoi convegni, che però si parlano di rado. Chi vuole studiare come le strategie di adattamento e mitigazione applicate alla rigenerazione urbana incidano sulla salute di chi abita le città trova poca letteratura da cui partire. Il gruppo di ricerca guidato da Ioanna Kyprianou, del Cyprus Institute, ha provato a misurare quanto poca, e a metterla in ordine: 110 pubblicazioni europee, estratte da un primo bacino di quasi 900, di cui una sola uscita prima del 2008 e metà dopo il 2018.

## Le strategie di mitigazione

Le strategie per ridurre le cause del problema si concentrano su un'area netta: progettazione urbana, pianificazione degli usi del suolo e contrasto diretto all'isola di calore, con 47 studi (**Figura 6**). ## Più forze che minacce, e perché

Classificando gli studi secondo lo schema SWOT emerge uno squilibrio marcato: 92 punti di forza e 96 opportunità, contro 40 debolezze e 35 minacce (**Figura 9**). Gli autori lo attribuiscono al modo in cui è costruita la ricerca: le query cercavano progetti di adattamento e mitigazione, non gli impatti negativi delle città sulla salute, e quindi il campione pende naturalmente verso ciò che funziona. È una precisazione onesta, e cambia il modo di leggere il resto: lo sbilanciamento non dice che la ricerca sia ottimista, dice che è stata cercata la parte ottimista.

## Quando una soluzione si ritorce contro

La parte più utile della discussione riguarda i casi in cui una strategia, applicata male, peggiora le cose. Le pavimentazioni ad alta riflettanza solare — spesso presentate come rimedio semplice ed economico contro il caldo — non aiutano nelle strade molto aperte, con ampia porzione di cielo visibile: lì la luce riflessa dal suolo non trova pareti che la assorbano e finisce sui passanti, alzando la temperatura media radiante, cioè la radiazione netta che il corpo scambia con le superfici intorno. Il risultato è un comfort peggiore nelle ore diurne proprio dove ci si aspettava un sollievo.

Il secondo caso è quello dei lock-in. Per il settore edilizio dell'Europa occidentale il potenziale di risparmio energetico stimato supera il 70 %, ma la quota che resta non realizzata — il rischio di lock-in — è intorno al 45 %: infrastrutture ereditate e tecnologie ad alta intensità di carbonio che persistono per decenni e di fatto ostacolano la transizione. Anche l'attuazione isolata di una singola misura, scollegata dalle altre, può amplificare i benefici oppure ridurli in modo sostanziale. La raccomandazione che ne deriva è di puntare su una progettazione urbana "malleabile", che riconosca l'impossibilità di prevedere con esattezza i bisogni futuri delle città e lasci margine di aggiustamento.

## Cosa la letteratura non dice

Alcune assenze sono esplicite. Il tema partecipativo — coinvolgere i cittadini nella co-progettazione degli spazi pubblici — è indicato dagli autori come il filone emergente più importante, eppure resta poco rappresentato negli studi e non compare tra le parole chiave frequenti. I problemi sanitari legati all'acqua, pur rilevanti per la vita urbana, non trovano quasi spazio: la letteratura analizzata li nomina ma non propone soluzioni.

Altri limiti sono metodologici e vanno tenuti presenti nel dare peso ai numeri. La revisione considera solo pubblicazioni in inglese, solo città europee, solo due banche dati (più una ricerca manuale), con l'ultimo aggiornamento a marzo 2022. La codifica SWOT è un'operazione qualitativa, quindi in parte soggettiva, e — come detto — la prevalenza di forze e opportunità è un effetto della costruzione delle query, non una proprietà del campo.

## Cosa se ne ricava

La raccomandazione principale che gli autori traggono è una sola, e la mettono in chiaro: promuovere nei progetti di rigenerazione un approccio guidato dalla comunità, inclusivo e partecipativo, così che le diverse vulnerabilità siano affrontate davvero e i diversi gruppi di popolazione ottengano benefici di salute equi. Le due cose che questo lavoro permette di fare in modo più concreto sono: sapere dove la ricerca è già densa e dove è quasi vuota — economia circolare, fiumi e foreste urbani, equità, dimensione sociale — e riconoscere che alcune soluzioni popolari, dalle pavimentazioni fredde alle misure applicate una alla volta, hanno un segno che dipende dal contesto e possono creare i lock-in che dovrebbero evitare.

**Piccolo glossario**

- *PRISMA*: protocollo standard per condurre e documentare una revisione sistematica della letteratura, articolato nei passaggi di identificazione, selezione e inclusione degli studi.
- *Logica CIMO*: schema che scompone una domanda di ricerca in Context (contesto), Intervention (intervento), Mechanism (meccanismo) e Outcome (esito); qui usato per costruire le query.
- *Analisi delle co-occorrenze*: tecnica bibliometrica che misura quanto spesso due termini compaiono insieme nei testi, per far emergere i temi centrali di un corpus.
- *Analisi SWOT*: classificazione di un tema o di uno studio in punti di forza, debolezze, opportunità e minacce; qui adattata come griglia di lettura critica anziché come strumento di pianificazione.
- *Isola di calore urbana*: il surriscaldamento delle aree edificate rispetto alle zone rurali circostanti, dovuto a materiali, geometria e attività delle città.
- *Lock-in*: situazione in cui scelte infrastrutturali o tecnologiche fatte oggi restano vincolanti per decenni e ostacolano transizioni successive, per esempio verso sistemi a basse emissioni.
