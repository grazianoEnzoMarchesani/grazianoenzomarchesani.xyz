The Local Climate Zone scheme is one of the few genuinely useful pieces
of standardisation in urban climatology. Seventeen classes — ten built,
seven natural — each defined by a handful of measurable parameters: how
much of the ground is covered by buildings, how tall they are, how much
sky you can see from the street, what the surfaces are made of. Two
researchers on opposite sides of Europe can say "LCZ 2, compact midrise"
and mean the same thing.

Then you point it at Ascoli Piceno and it starts to creak.

## The classes assume a city that was planned

The scheme was built on the morphology of cities that grew in blocks:
regular plots, comparable heights, streets that behave like canyons of
roughly constant section. A historic Italian centre is none of those
things. The building surface fraction is very high — often above 60% —
but the heights are irregular, the street widths change every twenty
metres, and the material is one thing: stone. In Ascoli, travertine.

Classified at the usual 100-metre resolution, most of the centre
collapses into a single class. Which is technically correct and
practically useless: it says the whole old town is one climate, when
anyone who has crossed Piazza del Popolo in August and then turned into
Via dei Soderini knows that it is not.

## Resolution is not a detail, it is the method

The paper we presented at *The Sustainable City XVII* argues that in
historic fabrics the LCZ grid has to go finer — down to the scale at
which a single street, a single courtyard, a single widening of the
section becomes its own unit. At that resolution the map stops being a
label and starts being a diagnosis: it shows the thermal spine of the
city, the pockets that never cool down at night, the few places where
the geometry already does the work that a design intervention would
otherwise have to do.

The cost of going finer is that you can no longer do it by hand. A
100-metre grid over a small town is a few hundred cells and an afternoon.
A 20-metre grid is tens of thousands, and every one of them needs sky
view factor, aspect ratio, surface fractions and roughness.

That is why [FETCH](/tools) exists. It reads open geospatial data,
derives the morphology parameters and assigns the class inside QGIS,
which turns a multi-week manual survey into something reproducible —
and, more importantly, into something someone else can check.

## What the map is for

An LCZ map is not an answer. It is a way of splitting a city into pieces
that behave differently, so that the questions that follow can be asked
in the right place: where do we plant, where do we de-pave, where would a
canopy change the mean radiant temperature by three degrees and where
would it change it by nothing.

In the work on minimum urban units this became explicit — the LCZ map is
the first cut, and the unit of adaptation is what you find inside it.

Travertine adds one more layer, and it is the one I am working on now.
The stone has a thermal behaviour that the standard LCZ material classes
do not describe well: high mass, high albedo, and a surface that has been
weathering for four centuries. A class that says "stone paving" is not
enough when the stone is the reason the city is the temperature it is.
