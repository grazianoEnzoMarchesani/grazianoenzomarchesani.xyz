There is a standard place in the process where microclimate analysis
happens. The design is essentially finished, the plan is fixed, the
sections are drawn, and someone runs a simulation to demonstrate that the
result is acceptable. If the numbers are bad, the response is a canopy
here and a lighter paving there.

This is not useless. It is just the least valuable moment at which it
could have been done.

## What the geometry has already decided

By the time a scheme is fixed, most of the microclimatic outcome is
locked. The orientation of the block, the width and direction of the
streets, the height ratio between the two sides of a canyon, the amount
of exposed thermal mass, whether the prevailing wind can get in at all —
these are the variables with the largest effect, and they are all decided
in the first fortnight of a project, usually on intuition.

What remains negotiable at the end — surface albedo, tree species,
shading devices — matters, but it operates in the margin left by the
geometry. Adding trees to a canyon whose section traps radiation is
buying back a fraction of what the section gave away.

## The obstacle is the turnaround time

The reason simulation lives at the end is not ignorance. It is that a
full ENVI-met run on a real urban model takes hours to prepare and hours
to compute, and the answer arrives after the design conversation has
moved on. A tool that answers in two days cannot participate in a
decision made in an afternoon.

So the practical problem is not "should we simulate earlier" — everybody
agrees — but "what can be answered fast enough to be part of the
conversation".

Most of the work I do on tooling comes from this. Building
[unicamTools](/tools) so a comfort study on a real square is an
afternoon's work rather than a specialist commission. Building the
[ENVI-met automation protocol](/tools) so the model preparation is a
scripted sequence instead of an artisanal one. Building
[EnviReader](/tools) so reading the output does not require the software
that produced it.

None of these makes the physics faster. They make everything around the
physics faster, which turns out to be where the days go.

## Coupling, and why it is the real argument

The chapter written with Naboni, Gherri, Di Nunzio, Maiullari, Cocci
Grifoni and Fiorito makes a stronger claim than "simulate earlier". It
argues that the building model and the microclimate model have to be
coupled, because each is a boundary condition for the other.

The building's energy demand depends on the microclimate outside its
envelope, which is not the airport weather file. The microclimate depends
on the surfaces, the waste heat and the geometry of the buildings. Run
separately, both models are solving the wrong problem with confident
precision.

Coupling them is technically awkward and computationally expensive, and
in practice it is done rarely. But the case for it is not perfectionism:
it is that the uncoupled error is systematic and always in the same
direction — it underestimates summer discomfort, in exactly the climate
scenarios where getting it wrong costs the most.

## What this means for teaching

If you accept the argument, the consequence for a design studio is
concrete. Environmental analysis cannot be a specialist course that
students take in parallel; it has to be a tool they reach for during the
design, at the resolution the design currently has.

Which is why the courses I teach start with a rough model and a bad
simulation in week one, rather than a good simulation in week ten. A bad
simulation of the right question, early, beats a good simulation of a
decision already taken.
