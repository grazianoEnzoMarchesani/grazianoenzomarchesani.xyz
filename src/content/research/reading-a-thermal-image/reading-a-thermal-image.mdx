A thermal camera is the most persuasive instrument in environmental
analysis, and that is its problem. The image comes out looking like
evidence: a façade in red, a shaded corner in blue, an unambiguous story
about where the heat is. Put it in a report and nobody asks a question.

They should, because at least four decisions were made between the
sensor and the picture, and none of them are visible in it.

## What the camera assumed on your behalf

**Emissivity.** The sensor measures radiance and converts it to
temperature using an assumed emissivity, typically 0.95. That is roughly
right for concrete, plaster and vegetation. It is wrong for polished
metal, wrong for glass at an angle, and wrong enough for wet surfaces
that a puddle can read several degrees off. A single global value applied
to a street scene containing all of these produces a map with errors that
vary by material — which is to say, errors that look like a pattern.

**Reflected apparent temperature.** Every surface reflects some of the
radiation around it. In an urban canyon at midday, part of what you read
on a shaded wall is the sunlit wall opposite. Unless the reflected
component was set from an actual measurement, it was set from a default.

**The scale.** The colour range is stretched between two values, and
those values are almost always chosen automatically per image. Two
photographs of the same street taken ten minutes apart can use different
scales, which means the colours are not comparable — a fact that survives
into surprisingly many published figures.

**The palette.** Iron, rainbow, and most of the other classic thermal
palettes are not perceptually uniform. They compress differences in some
parts of the range and exaggerate them in others, and they are largely
unreadable for red-green colour blindness. The visible boundary between
"orange" and "red" is a palette artefact, not a thermal one.

## Which is why the raw file matters

None of this is an argument against thermography. It is an argument for
working from the radiometric data rather than from the rendering.

A FLIR file carries the temperature matrix and the parameters used to
produce it. Once you can open that, the questions become answerable: what
is the actual difference between these two surfaces, what happens if I
correct the emissivity for this material, what is the distribution across
this region rather than the single value the cursor happened to land on.

That is what [Warmish](/tools) does — extract the matrix, expose the
parameters, let you interrogate the image pixel by pixel and export the
numbers. It exists because I needed it during the summer campaigns on
travertine surfaces, where the whole question was a few degrees of
difference between materials, and a few degrees is exactly the range in
which the defaults are unreliable.

## The field practice that goes with it

The tooling only helps if the measurement was taken properly. Three
things that make the difference, learned the slow way:

Take a reference. A surface of known emissivity in the frame — a piece of
matte tape works — gives you something to correct against.

Record the conditions. Air temperature, humidity, wind, cloud cover, time
to the minute. A thermal image without its context is a picture.

Shoot the same thing repeatedly. A single frame captures a moment of a
surface that is still exchanging heat. The interesting quantity is almost
never the peak — it is the cooling curve after sunset, which is where
thermal mass shows itself and where a stone city separates from a
concrete one.
